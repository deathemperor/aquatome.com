Relevant Information:
Contains only this single genus, Nematogenys, which contains a single species, N. inermis. Description: N. inermis has no adipose fin and and the caudal fin is short and rounded. Habitat: The genus is endemic to some rivers in Chile. The common name for this species is "Mountain Catfish". Previously in the Trichomycteridae family and is very rare in the aquatic hobby. There was a fossil which was found and described from the Miocene, continental fluviolacustrine deposits of the upper Cura–Mallín Formation (37–39°S), Chile (Azpelicueta & Rubilar, 1997). This species was named as Nematogenys cuivi. Etymology: The specific name inermis: Latin for unarmed, referring to absence of opercular spines, which distinguished it from presumed congeners in Trichomycterus (Trichomycteridae) at time of description. Remarks: There is not much information on keeping this species in the aquarium as this species has never been exported, due to their rarity.

IUCN Red List The species is presently considered extinct in almost all Chilean rivers with the exception of some relatively undisturbed coastal catchments (Arratia and Chang 1975, Habit et al. 2007, Vila and Habit 2015). Furthermore, various publications report that the species has undergone severe declines in abundances across its distribution range and only few remnant subpopulations exist (Arratia and Chang 1975, Arratia et al. 1981, Habit and Victoriano 2005, Vila and Habit 2015). The subpopulations seem to continue to decline. For example, its probable extinction has been recently documented in the middle course of the Biobío River (Habit et al. 2006). Furthermore, it was reported in Laguna Grande San Pedro in the Biobío Region (36° Latitude South) up to the nineties (Scasso and Campos 1998), but recent intensive monthly sampling that employed a variety of fishing techniques between March 2018 and February 2019 did not capture a single individual of the species in this location (Machino 2020). These changes may be attributed to water quality and habitat deterioration within its distribution range in recent decades (Habit et al. 2019). Habitat alteration due to water extraction, contamination and river regulation that have affected the abundance and distribution of the species are still in place and are expected to intensify in coming decades (Habit et al. 2006, 2019; Vila and Habit 2015; Díaz et al. 2021). Effects of these direct stressors will interact with projected climate change, as the species is endemic to the central regions of Chile that are highly vulnerable to climate (Araya-Osses et al. 2020, Habit et al. 2019). A population decline of 90% is suspected over the past 12 years. Therefore, this species is assessed as Critically Endangered.
