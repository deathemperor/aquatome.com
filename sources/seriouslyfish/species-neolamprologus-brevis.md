Neolamprologus brevis is a shell-dwelling cichlid found widely throughout Lake Tanganyika, and one of the commoner shell dwellers in the hobby. Where few snail shells collect, pairs share the same shell. Its territories are usually no more than 6" across but are defended vigorously. It is told apart from the similar Neolamprologus ocellatus by the vertical barring on its flanks.
## Distribution
Edit
Endemic to Lake Tanganyika. It is fairly widespread throughout the lake.
## Habitat
Write a note
Edit
It inhabits quite deep water around the shoreline in areas with a soft substrate, where the empty shells of snails collect. However, the areas in which N. brevis is found usually have fewer shells than the habitats of other shell dwelling species (see below).

## Maintenance
Write a note
Edit
The aquarium should have large open areas of sandy substrate, to which should be added a good number of empty snail shells (see breeding section below). More shells should be provided than there are individual fish. The substrate should be at least 2" deep as this species likes to dig. The water must be hard and alkaline.

## Diet
Write a note
Edit
Offer mainly live and frozen foods. Dried varieties are usually accepted, especially by tank-bred fish, but should not form the bulk of the diet.
## Behaviour and compatibility
Write a note
Edit
A territorial species that will defend its shell and the small territory around it vigorously. Due to its small size it can be combined with other species that inhabit different parts of the tank. Good tankmates include small rockdwellers such as the smaller Julidochromis species, and open water species such as Cyprichromis. Avoid Neolamprologus brichardi, because a breeding colony will kill shell dwellers in anything but a very large tank. If a number of individuals are kept together it will form a colony. If you're keeping it in this type of situation make sure there are enough shells to go around and try to buy more females than males.
## Sexual dimorphism
Write a note
Edit
Not easy to sex when young, but adult males are considerably larger than females.
## Reproduction
Write a note
Edit
Quite easy. Shell brooder. It may spawn in the community tank but if you want to raise a full brood, a separate tank should be used. Set it up as suggested above. Provide a plenty of snail shells, as the females will lay their eggs in these. Escargot shells are a good choice and can be obtained from most decent delicatessens. Water should be hard and alkaline with a pH of around 8.0-8.5 and a temperature of 25 – 26.5 °C77 – 80 °F. Keep several females per male and space the shells out a little. This helps to reduce aggression between males. Males also often spawn with multiple females if they are available. Condition the colony on a good diet of live and frozen foods. Females will attempt to catch the attention of males by displaying at the entrance of their chosen shells, which they bury until only the entrance is visible. When a male is sufficiently interested, the female swims into the shell where she deposits her eggs. When she has finished, she begins to back out of the shell at which point the male releases his sperm. This is 'sucked' into the shell by the action of the exiting female, thus fertilising the eggs. Alternatively, if the shell is large enough the male may enter it before releasing his sperm. After fertilisation the male plays no further part in brood care and is no longer welcome in the female's territory. The female sits on top of the shell, covering the entrance and fanning the eggs with her fins. These hatch in around 24 hours, the fry becoming free swimming at around the 6-7 day stage. Once free swimming they start to make forays away from the shell, venturing further and further as they grow, until eventually they are evicted by the female after another fortnight or so. The fry are large enough to accept brine shrimp nauplii or microworm once they become free swimming. It is probably better to remove them to a separate rearing tank at this stage to ensure the best survival rate, as although the parents do not usually harm them other fish in the colony may eat them.
## Notes
Edit
N. brevis is one of the most common species of shell dweller available in the hobby. Several colour morphs are available including "sunspot", "katabe" and "sambia".
As mentioned above, it is often found in areas within the lake where relatively few snail shells collect and in these areas, pairs of fish will share the same shell. This behaviour may be observed in the aquarium, depending on the number of shells provided and the individual fish.
The territories it establishes are very small, usually measuring no more than 6" across, but they are defended vigorously. The tiny fish will even bite hands or fingers that invade their 'personal space'! N. brevis can be distinguished from the similar Neolamprologus ocellatus by the vertical barring on its flanks. Both are often referred to as "Frog-faced" cichlids due to their bulbous facial features.
