Ageneiogarra imberba is a cyprinid without barbels from the upper Yangtze, Mekong and Red River basins in southern China, and the upper Red in Vietnam. Records from the Salween system result from confusion with the similarly-named Garra imberbis. With Garra micropulvinus it is placed in the Semilabeoina. It tends to inhabit swiftly-flowing headwaters and tributaries.
## Etymology
Edit
Ageneiogarra: from the Ancient Greek ἀγένειος (agéneios), meaning 'beardless', plus the generic name Garra, in reference to the lack of barbels.
imberba: from the Latin im-, meaning not', and berbis, meaning 'bearded', in reference to this species' lack of barbels.
## Distribution
Edit
Native to the upper Yangtze River (Jinshajiang), upper Mekong River (Lancangjiang) and upper Red River (Yuanjiang) basins in southern China and the upper Red River in Vietnam. Records from the Salween system are a result of confusion with the similarly-named congener G. imberbis (Vinciguerra, 1890).
Type locality is 'Min River, Kiating [Loshan], western Szechuan, China'.
## Habitat
Write a note
Edit
Tends to inhabit swiftly-flowing sections of headwaters and tributaries rather than larger, lowland river channels. The most favourable habitats contain clear, oxygen-saturated water which, allied with the sun, facilitates the development of a rich biofilm carpeting submerged surfaces.
## Notes
Edit
This species can be distinguished from the Garra and Ageneiogarra species inhabiting the Red River basin in Yunnan province, China, by the following combination of characters: no barbels; 46-52 lateral line scales; 16 circumpeduncular scales; pharyngeal teeth in 3 rows, 2.4.5-5.4.2; snout rounded, no secondary rostrum, no longitudinal groove in front of nostrils; anus close to pelvic-fin base.
The genus Garra is a particularly enigmatic grouping with new taxa described on a regular basis, while many of the existing ones may represent cases of misidentification or synonyms of other species. Some of the revisions have also been called into question, which has added further confusion. A full generic review would be ideal but is unlikely to materialise given the extensive distribution of its members which range from southern China across much of southeast Asia, India and the Middle East as far as north/central Africa.
Instead a number of less-extensive works published in recent years have resulted in a gradual, but continuing, improvement in knowledge, and it remains possible that the genus will be broken up into smaller taxa since the current assemblage is almost certainly polyphyletic.
Garra species are usually included in the subfamily Labeoninae/Cyprininae or tribe Labeonini (name varies with author) which by recent thinking is further divided into four subtribes; Labeoina, Garraina, Osteochilina, and Semilabeoina (Yang et al., 2012)[9]. The putatively monophyletic Garraina comprises a number of genetic lineages including Garra sensu stricto (which also includes Horalabiosa, Phreatichthys and possibly other genera), a small clade comprising Garra cambodgiensis and G. fascicauda (thus rendering Garra polyphyletic), Paracrossocheilus, and Gonorhynchus (which includes Akrokolioplax).
This species and Garra micropulvinus are placed in the Semilabeoina assemblage. The generic name Ageneiogarra Garman, 1912 was suggested for both, and the Catalog of Fishes and FishBase now place this species in Ageneiogarra. In addition, some genera which were previously considered to be close relatives of Garra species such as Discogobio, Discocheilus and Placocheilus, are now also placed in this subtribe.
All genera currently included in Garraina possess a lower lip modified to form a mental adhesive disc, allowing the fish to cling to surfaces in turbulent conditions. In most species the upper lip is almost entirely reduced and both the upper and lower jaw margins are keratinised, i.e., horny, and used to scrape food items from the substrate.
Garra species are distinguished from other Garraina members by the first two pectoral-fin rays usually being thickened, fleshy and unbranched, possession of 10-11 dorsal-fin rays, and a combination of internal characters. Some species have evolved particular environmental specialisms such as highly reduced eyes in hypogean forms or the ability to survive in thermal springs.
