Culturing infusoria at home is a relatively simple, cheap, and pretty straightforward process.
Use water from your aquarium, add some organic material to feed the infusoria, and wait for a few days until the infusoria multiplies. Once you see tiny white moving specks, they are ready to be fed to your fish fry.
In this article, I will provide a comprehensive overview of infusoria, including its nutritional value, and cultivation methods.
## What are Infusoria?
Right from the beginning I need to say that the term “infusoria” historically referred to a diverse group of microscopic aquatic organisms, primarily ciliates, found in infusions of decomposing organic material.
Infusoria is not a formal taxonomic category but rather a general term that includes various protozoans.
Infusoria are the microscopic single-celled animalcules belonging to Class, Ciliata, and Phylum, Protozoa. So, their taxonomy can vary widely depending on the specific organism.
## Infusoria as Live Food
Infusoria are an essential first food for fry and even some aquatic amphibians, particularly during their fry/larval stage.
For fish larvae at an early stage of development, infusoria are a universal first food. They are followed by small zooplankton like rotifers, and later by larger organisms such as daphnia, artemia, cyclops, etc.
Transitioning to mixed feeding is a critical period, requiring appropriately sized food in sufficient quantities. A lack of adequate food during this time can result in high mortality rates among the larvae.
Infusoria are small in size, soft-bodied, and highly nutritious! Additionally, these organisms have a high reproductive capacity. All these things make them an ideal starter diet for early-stage fish fry.
Infusoria can increase their biomass by up to 4 times in a single day, making them a promising food source for fish larvae.

## Nutrition Value of Infusoria
Infusoria encompass a wide variety of protozoan species, so their nutritional profiles can vary. For instance, Paramecium caudatum has a biochemical composition of:
- Protein: 58% of dry weight
- Fat: 32%
- Ash: 3%
- Caloric Value: 6.6 kcal/g of dry matter
## Description of Infusoria
- Size: Infusoria are quite small. They typically measure between 100 to 300 micrometers in length.
- Shape: Infusoria are generally oval or slipper-shaped, though their shape can vary slightly depending on the species.
- Surface: Their body is covered with longitudinal rows of numerous tiny cilia (hair-like structures), which are used for movement and feeding.
- Cilia: These cilia are evenly distributed over the surface of the cell, giving it a slightly hairy appearance and enabling it to move in a coordinated, spiral motion.
- Oral Groove: On one side of the cell, there is a depression known as the oral groove. They capture their food using their cilia, which help funnel the particles into the cell.
Interesting fact: Despite their tiny size, many species of Infusoria can exhibit impressive mobility. For example, Paramecium caudatum can travel at speeds of up to 2.5 mm per second at room temperature. This is almost 15 times their body length in just one second. It makes them 3 times faster than the world record holder for the 100-meter sprint.
## What do Infusoria eat in our Tanks?
Infusoria primarily feed on small microorganisms, including bacteria, other protozoa, algae, fungi, detritus, and dissolved organic matter.
Different types of bacteria and algae offer varying nutritional values. It is important to note that infusoria can filter and ingest any particles regardless of their nutritional value. Therefore, the presence of undesirable particles can negatively impact their productivity.
The development rate of infusoria is influenced by the quality and quantity of food. Studies have shown that a diverse bacterial diet results in a slightly higher division rate compared to a monoculture diet.
In aquariums, all kinds of bacteria can multiply rapidly. Infusoria feed on these bacteria, and when the bacteria grow uncontrollably, infusoria also proliferate, forming large, foggy white colonies.
Infusoria can consume up to 500% of their body weight in food daily. The efficiency of converting consumed food into growth ranges from 30-40%. This indicates that infusoria are generally quite effective at utilizing the food they consume.
## Reproduction of Infusoria
Infusoria reproduce primarily through cell division, though sexual reproduction can also occur in some species. In sexual reproduction, two cells conjugate, exchanging parts of their nuclear apparatus, which carries genetic material.
Under optimal conditions, freshwater infusoria reproduce very rapidly. For example, on average, they can divide 4-5 times a day at temperatures of 68 – 75°F (20 – 25°C). Whereas, marine infusoria generally reproduce slightly slower 1- 3 divisions per day.
When the temperature becomes too low or too high, the division rate decreases, eventually stopping entirely (around 32°F and at 95°F (0 – 35°C)).
## Where Is Infusoria Found?
I would say that most aquariums usually have infusoria. However, in a well-maintained tank, there will not be enough for newborn fry, so aquarium owners need to culture their own.
Note: Keep in mind that if hydrogen peroxide has been regularly used in the aquarium, it’s almost certain that any infusoria have been eliminated.
If you can’t access old aquarium water (or you simply don’t have infusoria), we can collect about 8.5 ounces (250 ml) of water from a long-standing puddle outside. Infusoria are found in almost any outdoor bodies of water (even puddles). Once home, pour it into your jar with aged tap water. Important: Be extremely cautious—along with infusoria, you could also introduce parasites and pests (such as hydra, planaria, dragonfly, damselfly nymphs. scuds, leeches, etc.). Caution is essential when using this method. Actually, I would not even recommend it.
## How to Culture Infusoria
- Starter Culture: Your old aquarium water. Note: We are particularly interested in the water from the bottom layer, right above the substrate. Additionally, water from a well-squeezed filter sponge is also a great addition for maintaining infusoria cultures).
- Container: Use a clean jar (1-2 gallons (4-8 liters) will be enough).
- Food Source: Add some nutrient materials.
- Light and Temperature: Keep the culture in a warm place with good ambient, fluorescent light or in a spot that receives natural sunlight.
- Harvesting: After a few days to a week, the water will become cloudy with infusoria, which can be fed to fry using a pipette or eyedropper.
- Substrate: No need.
- Air pump: No need.
Culturing infusoria can be a simple and effective way to ensure your aquarium fry have a reliable food source during their early growth stages.
Infusoria can easily tolerate huge temperature fluctuations ranging from of 50 – 86°F (10 – 30°C) and can survive in a wider pH range from 4.5 to 9.0. These organisms are also resilient to low oxygen concentrations in the water.Nonetheless, for optimal growth and reproduction, it is best to culture them at room temperature (around 68-77°F) and a pH range of 6.5 – 7.5.Infusoria are highly phototropic, meaning they are attracted to light and tend to congregate in well-lit areas. They also require ample oxygen to thrive. In aquariums with low oxygen levels, infusoria colonies will mostly be found near the water’s surface, where oxygen is more abundant.
## How To Feed Infusoria During Cultivation:
- Dried plant products: Small pieces of completely dried lettuce, carrot, or banana peel (about 1 inch (2.5 cm) in size).
- Skim milk: A few (3-5) drops of boiled milk. Use regular pasteurized milk, not the UHT milk in cartons, as the preservatives in long-life milk can harm the culture.
- Yeast: Mix 0.035 ounces (1 gram) of yeast per 20 gallons (80 liters) of water.
- Straw: Boil 0.35 ounces (about 10 grams) of hay in 1 quart (1 liter) of water for 15-20 minutes to kill unwanted bacteria. Let it sit at room temperature for a couple of days. Add 0.035 ounces (1 gram) of this infusion per 2.6 gallons (10 liters) of water.
Note: Infusoria cultured with hay infusion tend to be more resilient and larger.
Aquarists have successfully used a variety of materials to create and maintain infusoria cultures, including:
- Boiled rice.
- Liquifry
- Raw potato
- Powdered cereal, etc.
When feeding infusoria during cultivation, avoid adding too much food. Overfeeding can cause the water to spoil, which you’ll notice by a very foul odor.
So, a step-by-step process for culturing infusoria in home conditions looks like this:
- Fill the culture container with old aquarium water. The water should be at room temperature.
- Feed the infusoria with a culture medium.
- Place the container in a well-lit area for at least a week.
- Done.
If you want to keep the culture for some time, you can feed them with a drop of milk every two weeks or occasionally add a very small dry banana peel.
## Harvesting Infusoria
If everything is done correctly, the jar will turn cloudy (resembling milk) in the next few days. It indicates the growth of bacteria that infusoria feed on.
After about a week, the water should get clear, and you will see countless tiny white moving specks—these are the infusoria. The infusoria will multiply in such large numbers that, despite their microscopic size, they will become visible.
Thus it is time to start feeding the fry. Tiny fry need frequent feedings of infusoria until they are large enough to eat other foods like freshly hatched brine shrimp or commercial fry food.
- Siphon: Gently siphon a portion of the cloudy water, being careful to avoid the decaying nutrient material.
Tip: To concentrate infusoria at the surface, darken the jar while keeping the light at the surface. Never add water that contains infusoria directly into the fry tank. This water may not be clean and could be harmful to the fry.
- Add to Tank: Transfer the infusoria-rich water into the fry tank to provide them with a nutritious meal.
Tip: After collecting the infusoria, transfer them to a small container with a small amount of clean water. Allow them to stay there for a while to consume any bacteria that might have come with them. This ensures that you avoid introducing potentially harmful bacteria to the fry.
As the water with infusoria is used up, it’s important to replenish the jar with aged aquarium water to maintain the proper level.
## Storing Infusoria
Storying infusoria culture can be problematic in home conditions, thus, it is not recommended.
Overall, it’s best to use infusoria immediately after harvesting.
## In Conclusion
Infusoria are microorganisms that can be used as live food for a variety of aquatic animals.
Small, soft-bodied, prolific, and highly nutritious, infusoria are ideal as a starter diet for early fish larvae and often serve as their sole food in both freshwater and marine aquaculture.
