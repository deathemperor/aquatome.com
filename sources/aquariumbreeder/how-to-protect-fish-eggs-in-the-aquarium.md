Not looking for general fish reproduction facts? If you just need to know whether fish lay eggs or give live birth, this is not that article. Here we solve real aquarium problems: adult fish eating eggs, fungus, and how to save the clutch.
To protect fish eggs in a community aquarium, you must separate them from adult fish immediately. You have 24-48 hours before eggs start dying from fungus or predation. Most adult fish will eat their own eggs. Move the eggs to a dedicated rearing tank using a soft brush, or isolate the breeding pair in a tank breeder box. If you cannot move the eggs, use a spawning mop to give them a safe surface away from predators. No myths, just what works.

## How to Set Up a Breeder Box (Step by Step)
- Choose a breeder box that hangs inside the main tank. The mesh or slotted type allows water flow without letting predators in.
- Place it away from direct filter output. Strong flow can wash eggs through the slots.
- Gently transfer eggs using a turkey baster or soft brush. Do not touch them with fingers. The oils and bacteria from your skin can damage the egg membrane. I’ve seen entire clutches die after being handled with bare hands. Use a soft brush or baster instead.
- Add 2-3 drops of methylene blue (1% solution) directly into the breeder box. The water should be pale blue.
- Check twice daily for white (dead) eggs. Remove them immediately.
- After eggs hatch (24-72 hours), feed fry infusoria or vinegar eels inside the box. Do not release them until they are free-swimming (3-7 days).
How Do I Stop Them from Being Eaten?
There are two major methods to keep eggs from being eaten.
Method 1: Remove All Adult Fish
Move all adult fish (including parents, if they are known egg-eaters) to a separate tank. This guarantees zero predation. The downside: you need a second cycled aquarium.
Method 2: Use a Breeder Box or Divider
If you cannot move all fish, isolate the eggs inside a breeder box or behind a tank divider. This works best for egg-scattering species (tetras, barbs, danios).
What About the Parents?
Some parents eat their own eggs – especially first-time spawners. For species that typically care for eggs (angelfish, cichlids, discus), leave them with the eggs but watch closely. If they start eating them, remove the parents immediately. Reintroduce them after the fry hatch, or raise the fry separately.

## How to Identify Fertile vs. Infertile Fish Eggs
Not all eggs will hatch. Before you move anything, check if they’re actually fertile.
Fertile eggs:
- Translucent or slightly amber/brown (species dependent)
- You can see a tiny dark spot developing after 24-48 hours (the embryo)
- Stay firm and round
Infertile/dead eggs:
- Turn opaque white within 12-24 hours
- Develop a fuzzy white coating (fungus)
- Collapse or look deflated
The 48-hour rule: If an egg hasn’t shown any sign of development after 48 hours, it’s dead. Remove it immediately with a turkey baster before fungus spreads to healthy eggs.
Note: Some species (like corydoras) lay eggs that start off milky white and darken. Know your species before panicking.
## What to Do If Eggs Turn White (Fungus Treatment)
White, fuzzy eggs mean fungus. Here’s how to stop it from spreading.
Immediate actions:
- Remove all visibly white/fuzzy eggs with a clean pipette. Add methylene blue (1% solution) — 2-3 drops per gallon. The water should turn light blue.
- Increase aeration. Fungus thrives in stagnant water.
- Lower the temperature by 2°F (1°C) if your species allows it. Cooler water slows fungal growth.
What NOT to do:
- Do NOT use copper-based medications. They will kill the eggs.
- Do NOT do a large water change right now. Stability matters more than purity at this stage.
- Do NOT add antibiotics unless you’re 100% sure it’s a bacterial infection, not fungus.
When to cut your losses:
- If more than 50% of eggs turn white within 24 hours, the chances are low. But you can still try to remove the white ones and treat the rest with methylene blue before calling it a loss.
- If all eggs turn white within 12 hours → the male didn’t fertilize them, or water parameters are completely wrong. Check pH and temperature before the next spawn.
- If eggs develop fungus but some stay clear → remove the white ones, treat with methylene blue, and wait. You can still save the clutch.
## Species-Specific Protection Methods
Different fish need different strategies. Here’s what works for the most common aquarium breeders.
Egg-Scatterers (Tetras, Danios, Barbs)
These fish dump eggs everywhere and immediately eat them.
- Best method: Remove the parents immediately after spawning.
- Alternative: Use a spawning mop or mesh grid at the bottom. Eggs fall through, parents can’t reach them.
- Timeline: Eggs hatch in 24-36 hours at 78°F (25°C).
Adhesive Egg Layers (Angelfish, Discus, Cichlids)
These lay eggs on flat surfaces (glass, leaves, rocks) and often guard them.
- Best method: Leave the parents with the eggs IF they’re experienced breeders.
- When to intervene: If this is their first spawn, they’ll likely eat the eggs. Remove both parents after spawning.
- Fungus prevention: Add 2-3 drops of methylene blue (1% solution) per gallon to turn the water light blue. This stops fungus without harming the eggs.
Bubble Nest Builders (Bettas, Gouramis)
The male builds a foam nest at the surface and guards the eggs inside.
- Best method: Remove the female immediately after spawning (the male will attack her).
- Leave the male with the eggs — he’ll guard and maintain the nest.
- Do NOT disturb the nest. Even breaking one bubble can kill the eggs inside.
- Timeline: Fry hatch in 24-48 hours and stay in the nest for 3-4 days until they’re free-swimming.
Mouthbrooders (Some Cichlids, Arowana)
The parent holds eggs in their mouth for 2-4 weeks.
- Best method: Leave them completely alone.
- Critical mistake: Do NOT try to “check” the eggs by forcing the fish to open its mouth. This causes stress and the parent will swallow the eggs.

## Survival Factors in a Community Tank
There are three factors that will influence an egg’s survival in the main tank:
- Tank mates – any fish that eats eggs is a threat. Even if you feed them well, they may still go for the eggs. The only reliable solution is separation (breeder box or separate tank).
- Water quality and cleanliness – fungal infections kill more eggs than predators. On top of that, they can easily die out if the temperature or water content changes suddenly. You must keep all of this clean and consistent or you risk losing the entire brood! Keep your nitrites and ammonia at 0 ppm.
- Water flow and oxygenation — Eggs need constant oxygen exchange. Stagnant water kills them faster than predators. Use a gentle sponge filter in the rearing tank. Avoid strong currents from hang-on-back filters that can blast eggs off surfaces.
## 3 Common Mistakes That Kill Fish Eggs
Mistake #1: Moving eggs without matching water parameters
I’ve seen hobbyists scoop eggs from 82°F water into 76°F rearing tank. Eggs die within hours. Always match temperature, pH, and hardness exactly. Use water from the main tank for the rearing tank.
Mistake #2: Using an airstone that’s too strong
Bubble nests collapse. Adhesive eggs get blasted off leaves. Fry get pushed around. Use a sponge filter with a gentle flow, or no airstone at all for bubble nesters.
Mistake #3: Adding methylene blue after fungus has spread
Methylene blue prevents fungus. It doesn’t cure advanced infections. If eggs are already fuzzy, remove the dead ones immediately and treat the rest. Waiting 12 hours can kill the entire clutch.
## Before Spawning: Set Up for Success
You can’t protect eggs after they’re laid if your tank isn’t ready. Here’s what to do before the fish even mate.
Step 1: Identify the breeding pair
Not all fish in the tank will spawn. Watch for chasing, cleaning behavior, or color changes. Separate the suspected pair into a breeding tank 2-3 days before expected spawning.
Step 2: Add spawning surfaces
- For egg-scatterers (tetras, danios): Add a spawning mop or a layer of marbles at the bottom.
- For adhesive egg layers (angelfish, cichlids): Place a flat slate, broad leaf, or a spawning cone.
- For bubble nest builders (bettas, gouramis): Provide floating plants or a piece of styrofoam cup.
Step 3: Condition the parents
Feed high-quality live or frozen foods (bloodworms, brine shrimp) for 1-2 weeks before spawning. Well-fed parents produce more eggs and are less likely to eat them immediately.
Step 4: Set up a rearing tank
Have a separate 5-10 gallon tank cycled and ready. Use a sponge filter (no hang-on-back intake that can suck up fry). Keep the water temperature and chemistry identical to the main tank.
Step 5: Prepare methylene blue and tools
Buy a 1% methylene blue solution and a turkey baster before you need them. When eggs appear, you won’t have time to shop.
## After Hatching: First Foods for Fry
Once eggs hatch, you have 24-48 hours before fry need food. They absorb their yolk sac first.
Day 1-3: Yolk sac (no feeding needed)
Day 3-7: Infusoria or Rotifers
Both are microscopic live foods that fry can eat. You can culture vinegar eels in a jar with apple cider vinegar and water.
Day 7+: Vinegar eels, Microworms, Moina
This is the gold standard.
Avoid powdered dry foods — they foul the water and kill fry.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### How long does it take for fish eggs to hatch?
Most freshwater fish eggs hatch in 24-72 hours depending on species and temperature. At 78°F (25°C), tetra and danio eggs hatch in ~24 hours. Angelfish eggs take 48-72 hours. Bettas hatch in 24-48 hours.
### Can fish eggs survive in a community tank?
Rarely. In a community tank with adult fish, less than 5% of eggs survive without intervention. Predation is the #1 killer. You need to separate the eggs or the adults.
### Should I remove fish eggs from the main tank?
Yes, if you want them to hatch. Move them to a dedicated rearing tank using a soft brush or turkey baster. Keep the water parameters identical to the main tank to avoid shock.
### Why are my fish eating their own eggs?
This is normal behavior for most species. In the wild, parents recoup energy by eating some eggs. In captivity, first-time spawners often eat the entire clutch. Experienced breeders (like some cichlids) usually guard them properly.
### What do I feed fish eggs?
Nothing. Eggs don’t eat. Once they hatch into fry (free-swimming after 3-7 days depending on species), feed them infusoria, vinegar eels, or freshly hatched baby brine shrimp.
