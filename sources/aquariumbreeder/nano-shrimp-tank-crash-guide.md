A 5-gallon tank is not just a smaller version of a 20-gallon. It’s a completely different biological environment. In a large tank, water volume dilutes your mistakes. In a nano tank, a single dead snail or a pinch of excess food turns into a toxic bomb in 12 hours. If your nano tank is crashing, it’s not bad luck. It’s fluid dynamics.

## The Nano Tank Emergency Checklist
Your shrimp are dying or acting erratic. Do this now:
- Test Ammonia immediately. If >0.25 ppm, perform a 50% water change using temperature-matched, dechlorinated water (Seachem Prime is mandatory here). Yes, large changes are stressful (and may cause the White Ring of Death), but ammonia is a guaranteed death sentence. Follow up with 10-15% changes daily until it hits 0. Alternatively, do 10-15% water changes every 4-6 hours until ammonia hits 0. The same total volume gets exchanged, but with a bit less osmotic shock.
- Test TDS. If >300 (Neocaridina) or >200 (Caridina), dilute it slowly. Do a 15% water change with pure, remineralized RO water (or distilled water). Repeat every 2 days until TDS drops to the safe zone. Do not use tap water for top-offs – read my complete TDS
- Check Temperature. If it swung >4°F (2°C) in 24 hours, stabilize it with a reliable 25W adjustable heater. Do not add ice directly; it causes localized thermal shock.
- Remove the source. Siphon out any dead shrimp, uneaten food, or decaying plant matter immediately. In a 5-gallon, a single dead snail is a biological weapon.
- Zero additions. Do not add any new livestock, fertilizer, or food for at least 2 weeks. Let the biofilter recover.
## The 3 Reasons Nano Tanks Fail (With Hard Data)
- The TDS Swing of Death (Evaporation)
In a 5-gallon (20-liter) tank, evaporation of 0.5 gallons (2 liters) per week (common in dry climates) concentrates your TDS by 10-20%. If you start at 180 TDS, after one week of top-offs with tap water, you’re at 200-220 TDS. After a month, you’re at 300+ TDS. Shrimp will have hard times molting at 350 TDS.
The fix: Never use tap water for top-offs in a nano tank. Use RO water only. Tap water adds minerals that don’t evaporate – only pure water brings TDS down.
Warning: If you use pure RO water for top-offs and water changes, you are stripping the water of GH (General Hardness). If your GH drops too low, your shrimp cannot build a new shell and molt properly. Always remineralize your RO water for water changes.
2. The 12-Hour Ammonia WindowA 20-gallon (40-liter) tank gives you about 3 days to notice an ammonia spike. A 5-gallon tank gives you 12-24 hours. A forgotten piece of blanched zucchini or a dead bladder snail will spike ammonia to lethal levels before you even smell it.
The fix: Test ammonia every 3-5 days. If you see a spike, do multiple small water changes (10-15% every 4-6 hours) – not one big change.
Read my article “Ammonia In Shrimp Tanks” for the full science.
- Temperature Whiplash
Small water volume loses heat instantly. In a 5-gallon (20-liter) tank, a room temperature drop from 72°F (22°C) to 68°F (20°C) will drop the tank temperature in 60-90 minutes at most. Shrimp survive really bad sudden temperature swings of more than 4°F (2°C) in a day.
The fix: Get a 25W adjustable heater with a built-in thermostat. Do not rely on room temperature. Read my temperature guides [link to temperature breeding article] [link to cooling article].
## The Physics of Small Water Volume (Why Nano Is Different)
- Surface area to volume ratio. A standard 5-gallon tank has roughly 200 square inches of surface area. A 20-gallon (40-liter) long has about 400 square inches. But the volume is 4x larger. Result: gas exchange (oxygen in, CO2 out) is proportionally worse in nano tanks. That’s why oxygen crashes happen faster, and CO2 buildup becomes lethal sooner.
- Thermal mass. Water holds heat. 20 gallons of water at 72°F (22°C) takes 4-6 hours to drop 2°F in a 65°F (18°C) room. 5 gallons takes 45-60 minutes. Same for heating – a 100W heater will overshoot in a nano tank and cook your shrimp. Use 10-25W only, and always with a thermostat.
- Evaporation rate. Per gallon of surface area, a nano tank loses water 2-3x faster than a standard tank. That’s why TDS creep is your number one enemy. Top off with RO water only – no exceptions.
## The “It Fits Here” Trap (Location Matters More in Nano Tanks)
When people buy a 5-gallon tank, they usually pick its location based on one rule: *where does it fit?* It goes on a desk near a window, under an AC vent, or next to a radiator.
In a 5-gallon nano tank, direct sunlight will cook your shrimp in 1 hour and trigger a MASSIVE algae bloom. An AC vent dropping cold air at night will easily cause a 5°F (3°C) temperature swing while you sleep, triggering unprepared molts and the White Ring of Death.
Nano tanks have zero thermal mass. They react to the room instantly.
The fix: Place your nano tank in an interior room, away from windows, vents, and doors. If you can’t control the room temperature, you must use an aquarium fan for cooling and a thermostat-controlled heater for warming.
Immediate Action: If your tank is near a window or AC vent, move it. If you can’t, install a temperature controller. Do not skip this step.

## The Invisible Killers in Nano Tanks
In a 40‑gallon breeder, a drop of tap water or a cheap decorative rock is a rounding error. In a 5‑gallon tank, it’s a lethal dose. Nano tanks magnify chemical mistakes.
- The chloramine trap.Many municipal water supplies use chloramine, not chlorine. It does not evaporate. If you do top‑offs or water changes without a dechlorinator that neutralizes chloramine (like Seachem Prime), you’re slowly poisoning the colony.
- Then there’s copper. Cheap colored gravel, non‑aquarium‑safe glues, or even your tap water can leach it. Shrimp are invertebrates – copper destroys their hemocyanin (blood). In a small volume, the concentration builds up fast. Always use inert, aquarium‑safe substrates and decorations.
- Flea treatments on pets– dogs, cats – transfer to your hands, then to the tank. In a 5‑gallon, that’s enough to kill the colony. Wash your hands thoroughly before any tank work.
- CO2 injection is Russian roulette in a nano tank.A 1 bubble per second in a 20‑gallon (80‑liter) is safe. The same rate in a 5‑gallon raises CO2 to 30+ ppm in hours – lethal. If you inject CO2, use a drop checker and keep the rate at 0.5 bubble per 3‑5 seconds. Better yet, don’t inject CO2 in a nano shrimp tank at all. See also: CO2 in a Shrimp Tank
- Never use hot tap water for water changes.Hot water reacts with copper pipes. In a 5‑gallon, even trace copper is lethal. Always use cold water and dechlorinate it.
- Finally, the over‑cleaning trap.Nano tank owners panic‑clean. They scrub algae, vacuum gravel thoroughly, rinse filter media in tap water. Each of these destroys beneficial bacteria. In a 20‑gallon, you can lose 20% of bacteria and recover in days. In a 5‑gallon, losing 20% means ammonia spikes to 0.5 ppm within 24 hours. Clean sparingly. Never clean filter media and substrate in the same week. Never rinse filter media in tap water – use tank water only.

## Nano Tank Success Checklist (Do This Every Week)
To keep a nano tank stable, follow this routine:
1. Monday: Feed them with small amount of flakes or pellets. Test TDS. If >250, top off with RO water only. No remineralization for top-offs.
2. Wednesday: Test ammonia. If >0, do a 10-15% water change with remineralized RO water.
3. Friday: Feed a varied, tiny meal (a pinch of Bacter AE for shrimplets, or a small piece of blanched kale/zucchini). Crucial: Remove any uneaten food after 4 hours. In a nano tank, rotting food is an ammonia bomb. (Read my full guide on [how often and how much to feed shrimp]. Underfeed rather than overfeed.
4. Sunday: 15% water change with remineralized RO water. Vacuum only one small corner of the substrate – not the whole bottom.
Never:
- Use tap water for top-offs.
- Add new shrimp without drip acclimation for at least 2 hours.
- Leave uneaten food for more than 2 hours.
- Clean filter media and substrate in the same week.
## The Nano Tank Biofilm Paradox (And The “Algae Rock” Hack)
Here is the catch: shrimp need biofilm and algae to survive between feedings. If you don’t feed them, they need to graze on natural food.If you don’t feed them, they should be grazing. But you cannot intentionally grow algae inside a 5-gallon nano tank. In a 20-gallon, algae is just an eyesore.
In a 5-gallon, algae mean your nutrients are spiking. More importantly, algae consume oxygen at night. In a nano tank, this causes a massive pH drop and oxygen depletion while you sleep, also triggering molting issues. So how do you provide natural food without crashing the water parameters? You grow it outside.
### The “Algae Rock” Hack:
- Take a spare container (a bucket or a large bottle) and fill it with old tank water from your water changes.
- Drop in a few clean, aquarium-safe rocks or ceramic filter media.
- Place the container in a warm spot under strong light (or direct sunlight) and add a tiny pinch of fish food or fertilizer to feed the algae.
- In 7–10 days, the rocks will be covered in safe, green biofilm and algae.
- Move these rocks into your nano tank. Your shrimp will graze them clean in a day. Swap them back with “dirty” rocks from your outside container.This gives your shrimp the exact natural diet they need (preventing starvation and survival issues) without risking a single parameter swing in your main tank.

### The Easy Way: Leaves and Wood
Before you set up an outdoor algae bucket, try the easiest biofilm hack: Indian Almond Leaves (Catappa leaves) or Alder Cones. Drop one or two into your nano tank. As they break down, they release tannins (lowering pH slightly) and create a massive surface area for microorganisms and biofilm to grow. Your shrimp will graze on them 24/7, and the leaves provide essential hiding spots for shrimplets to avoid adults.
## When to Give Up on Your Nano Tank
If you’ve lost three colonies in six months, switch to a 10-gallon. It’s not you. It’s the physics of small water volume. Some people succeed with nano tanks. Most don’t. I’ve kept a 3-gallon jar alive for 18 months, but I tested parameters every other day and did very small water changes twice a week. To be honest, it felt like a chore and I do not want to do it again. So, if you can’t commit to that, don’t start small.
