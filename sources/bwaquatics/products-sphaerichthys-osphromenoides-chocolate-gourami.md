This is not a general community fish.Sphaerichthys osphromenoides (Chocolate Gourami)The Chocolate Gourami is a labyrinth fish restricted to peat swamps and blackwater streams of Peninsular Malaysia, Sumatra and Borneo. Its home water is warm, extremely soft and acidic, under dense forest canopy with deep leaf litter, submerged branches and tannins. Peaceful but delicate, it does best in a mature, quiet blackwater aquarium with excellent water quality and carefully chosen tankmates.Physical Characteristics Body Shape: Deep, laterally compressed body with a pointed head. Fins: Rounded fins; red, blue or cream edging may develop depending on population and condition. Coloration: Rich chocolate-brown with pale cream to golden vertical bands. Habitat: Warm, shaded peat swamp streams and pools. Care GuideTemperament: Peaceful but timid. Keep in a group of about 4 to 6 or more with plenty of cover. Good with Boraras, Sundadanio, small rasboras, kuhli loaches and gentle Parosphromenus. Avoid large, aggressive, very active or competitive feeders, fin-nippers and territorial gouramis. A species-focused tank is best for acclimating or breeding.Max Size: About 2 to 2.4 in total lengthTank: Leaf litter, botanicals, driftwood, floating plants, dim light and gentle filtration. Use a tight lid to keep warm, humid air above the water for its labyrinth breathing. Add only to a biologically mature aquarium.TDS: Below 100 ppmpH: 4.0 to 6.5Temperature: 77 to 84°FDiet: Micro-predator that may refuse prepared foods at first. Live or frozen daphnia, cyclops, baby brine shrimp, mosquito larvae, grindal worms, small bloodworms and soft micro granules. Feed small portions once or twice daily and make sure shy fish eat.Health: Very sensitive to ammonia, nitrite, high nitrate and sudden changes in pH, hardness or temperature. Watch each fish for feeding, body condition, odd coloring or hiding. Avoid rearranging the tank, strong current and bright light.Breeding: Mouthbrooder. About 20 to 40 eggs are incubated in the male's mouth for around two weeks. Older references often name the female as the brooder, but FishBase identifies the male.
