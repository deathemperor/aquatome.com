Before it was named it was traded as Betta sp. "Laut". It is closely related to Betta patoti and the two can be hard to tell apart. In ideii the iridescence is more spread out and the scaling looks more numerous, and males show an orange patch in front of the eye running into the upper lip, plus a white vertical patch on the lower lip.Physical Characteristics Body Shape: Long and slender with a large, blunt head. Fins: Long anal fin and rounded to pointed caudal fin. Males have longer fins than females. Scaling: Iridescent scaling across the whole body, more widespread than in Betta patoti, including blue scales on the cheeks and lower jaw. Coloration: Dark brown body with blue iridescence, an orange patch in front of the eye and a white patch on the lower lip in males. Females are duller. Habitat (wild Betta ideii): Presumed to be clear, flowing forest streams like other unimaculata-complex species; no water measurements have been published. Care GuideTemperament: Aggressive. Best kept in a species-only tank, since they will eat any fish that fits in their mouth. Give a pair plenty of caves and cover to break line of sight. Recommended 1 pair per 10 gal, or 20 gal for breeding.Max Size: 4 to 4.5 inchesTDS: 60 to 100pH: 5.0 to 7.0Temperature: 72 to 82°F (they prefer cooler water, but warmer water brings out color and breeding behavior)Diet: Carnivorous. We currently feed live blackworms, frozen bloodworms and pellets.Health: In our experience, fish in this complex are very prone to ich and velvet during the first few weeks after import, so watch new arrivals closely.Breeding: Paternal mouthbrooder. The female collects the eggs during spawning and passes them to the male, who holds them in his mouth for about 13 to 18 days. Soft, acidic water helps trigger spawning. Keep the tank quiet and leave a holding male alone, since a disturbed male may swallow or spit out the brood. He releases fully formed fry that can eat baby brine shrimp right away.
