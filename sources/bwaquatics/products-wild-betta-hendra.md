It belongs to the coccina complex but looks very different from the red species: males are dark with bright blue-green iridescence across the flanks. It can be told apart from its neighbor Betta uberis by its 10 to 12 dorsal-fin rays (uberis has 14 to 17).Physical Characteristics Body Shape: Slender and elongated, one of the larger species in the coccina complex. Fins: Rounded caudal fin and a long anal fin running most of the body length. Males have longer, more pointed unpaired fins than females. Scaling: Small, even scales. On males, blue-green iridescence covers most of the flank scales, so the body looks evenly metallic rather than spotted. Coloration: Males are dark reddish-brown to black with blue-green iridescence on the flanks and fins and a black throat. Females are smaller, browner and much less iridescent. Habitat (wild Betta hendra): Shaded peat swamp pools and streams in the Sabangau basin. At the type locality the water measured around pH 4.0 with almost no dissolved minerals. Care GuideTemperament: Moderately aggressive. Males can be territorial when setting up a breeding site, but because of their small size you can keep multiple pairs with enough space and cover. Recommended 1 pair per 5 gal.Max Size: 1.7 to 1.8 inchesTDS: 60 to 100pH: 4.0 to 6.0Temperature: 75 to 82°FDiet: Carnivorous. We currently feed live blackworms, frozen bloodworms and pellets.Breeding: Bubblenester. The male builds a small bubble nest at the surface, usually under a floating leaf, inside a floating cave or among plants, and places the eggs in it after spawning. Pairs can often stay together in a well-planted tank; remove the female if the male keeps chasing her. Eggs hatch in 24 to 48 hours, and the fry are free-swimming about 3 days later.
